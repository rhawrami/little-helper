# Academic and Working-Paper Literature Measuring the Effects of LLMs / AI Coding Assistants on Open Source Software (2022 to Nov 2026)

Scope note: this file catalogues primary empirical papers (preprints and peer-reviewed). Status flags: **[PR]** = peer-reviewed/published, **[WP]** = working paper / preprint (not peer-reviewed as far as verified), **[VEN]** = vendor or foundation report (not peer-reviewed; mentioned only briefly). Version dates are the arXiv/SSRN dates seen on 2026-11-12. Items marked "(not re-fetched this session)" are well-known papers whose figures come from their abstracts as previously known; they should be spot-checked before publication.

## Q1. What causal / quasi-experimental studies exist on Copilot, ChatGPT and agentic tools and OSS productivity?

### Takeaway
A sizeable quasi-experimental literature now exists (roughly 12 core papers), using four main identification levers: (a) **Copilot's language-selective launch** (Python supported, R not), (b) **GitHub's free-Copilot eligibility threshold for top maintainers** (regression discontinuity), (c) **staggered tool adoption detected from repo artifacts** (`.cursorrules`, `CLAUDE.md`, agent-authored PRs, `Co-authored-by: Claude` trailers) analysed with modern staggered DiD, and (d) **access shocks** (Italy's 2023 ChatGPT ban; country-level restrictions). Almost all find **positive but modest, often transient, gains in output** (roughly +2% to +40% depending on unit and metric), with costs showing up in **coordination/review time, code complexity and static-analysis warnings**, and with benefits **unevenly distributed between core and peripheral contributors**. No paper focuses on foundational, decades-old infrastructure projects.

### Cited Findings

**(a) Copilot language-support natural experiment**
- **Yeverechyahu, Mayya & Oestreicher-Singer, "The Impact of Large Language Models on Open-source Innovation: Evidence from GitHub Copilot"** [WP; arXiv v1 12 Sep 2024, v4 24 May 2026; no journal ref listed, JEL codes only]. Identification: Copilot's October 2021 launch supported languages such as Python (and Rust) but not R or Haskell; projects in supported vs unsupported languages are compared. Outcomes: contributions split into "substantive/capability" (new functionality) vs "incremental/iterative" (maintenance, refinement). Findings (v4 abstract): Copilot availability **raised OSS contributions by 28 to 40 percent**; incremental contributions rose significantly more than substantive ones in all specifications; the gap was **larger in more active projects** and **widened after the June 2022 model upgrade**. Authors suggest policy might steer toward higher-value innovation. — [arXiv 2409.08379 (latest)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.08379); [v3 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.08379v3)

**(b) Regression discontinuity on GitHub's top-maintainer free-access program**
- **Hoffmann, Boysel, Nagle, Peng & Xu, "Generative AI and the Nature of Work"** [WP; NBER conference draft Jul 2024 titled "Generative AI and Distributed Work: Evidence from Open Source Software"; CESifo WP 11479, Nov 2024; HBS WP 25-021; 2025 revision circulating]. Identification: GitHub gave free Copilot to developers above an internal "top maintainer" ranking threshold; RD compares maintainers just above vs just below. Sample: "linchpin" OSS maintainers. Findings: Copilot access shifts task allocation **toward core coding and away from project management**; HBS summary reports **coding activity +12%** and **project-management/admin work −25%** relative to non-access group; mechanisms are **more autonomous (less collaborative) work** and **more exploration vs exploitation**; effects **larger for lower-ability individuals**; effects strongest in year one but persist over two years. — [CESifo WP 11479 (PDF)](https://www.ifo.de/DocDL/cesifo1_wp11479.pdf); [NBER conference draft](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f207681.pdf); [HBS Working Knowledge summary (12%/25%)](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/can-ai-help-managers-love-their-jobs-again); [GitHub blog interview (persistence over 2 years)](https://github.blog/news-insights/policy-news-and-insights/inside-the-research-github-copilot/)

**(c) Staggered adoption using proprietary or artifact-detected usage**
- **Song, Agarwal & Wen (UT Austin), "The Impact of Generative AI on Collaborative Open-Source Software Development: Evidence from GitHub Copilot"** [arXiv v1 2 Oct 2024; v2 Jul 2025; v3 May 2026; v4 Aug 2026; an INFORMS *Information Systems Research* DOI 10.1287/isre.2024.1361 exists for this title, but the page returned 403 so final publication status was not verified]. Data: **GitHub's proprietary Copilot usage data** linked to public OSS repo data, 2021–2022; identification: staggered Copilot adoption with **generalized synthetic control**. Findings (v2 abstract): **project-level code contributions +5.9%**, **individual code contributions +2.1%**, **developer coding participation +3.4%**, but **coordination time for code integration +8%** (more code discussion); **peripheral developers gain less and see larger coordination-time increases than core developers** (attributed to lower project familiarity). v1 reported project-level productivity +6.5%. — [arXiv 2410.02091v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02091v2); [ISR DOI page](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/full/10.1287/isre.2024.1361)
- **Xu, Medappa, Tunc, Vroegindeweij & Fransoo, "AI-Assisted Programming Decreases the Productivity of Experienced Developers by Increasing the Technical Debt and Maintenance Burden"** [WP; arXiv econ.GN v1 11 Oct 2025, v3 28 Jan 2026; presented WITS 2025, CIST 2025, SCECR 2025, INFORMS 2024]. Setting: OSS developer activity after Copilot's introduction (DiD design per secondary description). Findings: productivity rises overall but **gain driven by peripheral (less experienced) developers**; post-adoption code needs **more rework** to meet repo standards; **core developers review 6.5% more code but their original code productivity falls 19%**. Authors frame it as a maintenance burden concentrating on a shrinking pool of experts. — [arXiv 2510.10165](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.10165)
- **He, Miller, Agarwal, Kästner & Vasilescu (CMU / UVic), "Does AI-Assisted Coding Deliver? A Difference-in-Differences Study of Cursor's Impact on Software Projects"** [WP; arXiv v1 6 Nov 2025, v3 26 Jan 2026; venue not listed on arXiv]. Sample: **807 GitHub repos (≥10 stars, non-fork) adopting Cursor Jan 2024–Mar 2025** (detected via committed `.cursorrules` / `.cursor` files, dated from git history) vs **1,380 propensity-matched never-adopters** (GHArchive lagged covariates, same primary language); monthly panel Jan 2024–Aug 2025; **Borusyak et al. (2024) imputation DiD**. Outcomes: commits, lines added; SonarQube warnings, duplicate-line density, cognitive complexity. Findings: **lines added +281% in month 1, +48% in month 2, +28.6% average, not sustained beyond ~2 months**; commits +55% month 1, no significant average effect; **static-analysis warnings +29.7% (persistent)**; **code complexity +40.7% (persistent; the discussion section reports 25.1%, an internal inconsistency)**; duplication n.s. Panel GMM: a 100% rise in complexity is associated with a 64.5% fall in lines added (warnings: −50.3%), i.e. quality debt drives later velocity slowdown. Limitations: config-file adoption is intent-to-treat; concurrent tools (Copilot, Claude, Windsurf, Cline) contaminate controls, biasing toward zero; model backend unknown; TWFE complexity estimate fails pre-trend test; sample skews JS/TS/Python; no heterogeneity by project age/size/stars reported. — [arXiv 2511.04427v2 (HTML)](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.04427v2); [abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.04427v2)
- **Agarwal, He & Vasilescu, "AI IDEs or Autonomous Agents? Measuring the Impact of Coding Agents on Software Development"** [arXiv v1 20 Jan 2026, v2 27 Jan 2026; an ACM DL entry exists (DOI 10.1145/3793302.3793589), consistent with MSR 2026 proceedings, not verified]. Data: AIDev dataset; adoption = repo's first agent-generated PR; staggered DiD with matched controls; monthly velocity and quality outcomes. Findings: **"agent-first" repos (agents are the first observable AI tool) see large, front-loaded velocity gains; "IDE-first" repos (prior Cursor-style usage) see minimal or short-lived gains** (diminishing returns); in both, **static-analysis warnings rise ~18% and cognitive complexity ~39%**, i.e. sustained agent-induced technical debt. — [arXiv 2601.13597](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.13597); [ACM DL PDF](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3793302.3793589)
- **Xu, Cui, Ye & Zhou (Peking University), "Decoupling Code Complexity from Newcomer Participation: A Causal Study of AI Coding Agent Adoption in OSS"** [WP; arXiv 2 Jul 2026]. Sample: **1,888 projects adopting an agent (Cursor, Claude Code, etc.) identified by first commit of a config file**; main analysis on **603 adopters with genuine pre-adoption history** vs matched non-adopters; DiD with multiple estimators. Findings: **no significant decline in newcomer inflow**, onboarding or retention; good-first-issue tasks not reduced (sparse, correlational); **Python cognitive complexity +~11%** (about a quarter of He et al.'s estimate), **cyclomatic complexity +3–4% across languages**. Limitations: config file as proxy; most conservative trend spec shows slight insignificant newcomer dip; findings for "established projects". — [arXiv 2607.01810](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.01810)
- **Quispe (Caltech), "Coding Beyond Your Training: Claude Code and the Technological Frontier of Software Developers"** [WP, "preliminary draft", May 2026, econ.GN]. Sample: **5,838 GitHub developers** (3,060 early Claude Code adopters with first `Claude`-co-authored commit in Q2–Q3 2025; 2,778 later adopters as not-yet-treated controls); 163,464 developer-months Jan 2024–Apr 2026; **Callaway–Sant'Anna** doubly robust staggered DiD. Findings: at event time 0, **monthly commits +40.7 (simple ATT +20.8) vs pre-mean 21.3**; **languages used +0.83 (pre-mean 0.63)**; repos +1.5; newly used languages +0.31. Author states identification limits prevent a strict causal claim (reverse causation: starting new-language projects and installing Claude for them; pre-adoption activity uptick). — [arXiv 2605.25438](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.25438v1)
- **Daniotti, Wachs, Feng & Neffke, "Who is using AI to code? Global diffusion and impact of generative AI"** [PR: *Science* 2026, vol. 391, no. 6787, pp. 831–835; arXiv preprint June 2025]. Method: trained classifier to detect AI-assisted Python functions; applied to **>30 million contributions** to OSS Python projects (preprint: 80M commits, ~200k developers; published version: all commits by ~100k US GitHub users plus cross-country comparisons: US, China, France, Germany, India, Russia); within-developer panel. Findings: by end-2024 **~29% of Python functions written by US developers had substantial AI support**; US lead narrowing; AI use **raised quarterly output (online code contributions) ~3.6%**; **productivity gains accrue mainly to experienced developers**, even though newcomers use AI more intensively (press summaries cite ~37% vs ~27% AI share; not verified in primary text). — [arXiv 2506.08945v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.08945v2); [CSH news release](https://csh.ac.at/news/ai-is-already-writing-almost-one-third-of-new-software-code/); [Corvinus summary: "Generative AI gives seasoned developers the edge"](https://www.uni-corvinus.hu/research/generative-ai-gives-seasoned-developers-the-edge/?lang=en); [Utrecht Univ. news](https://www.uu.nl/en/news/productivity-of-ai-use-in-software-coding-not-equal-among-users)

**(d) Access shocks: Italy's ChatGPT ban and country restrictions**
- **Kreitmeir & Raschky (Monash), "The Unintended Consequences of Censoring Digital Technology – Evidence from Italy's ChatGPT Ban"** [WP, Apr 2023; SoDa Labs WP]. Hourly GitHub output of **>8,000 professional users** in Italy vs two other European countries; DiD. Findings: **Italian output fell ~50% in the first two business days** after the ban, then returned to normal; VPN/Tor use rose (circumvention). — [arXiv 2304.09339](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.09339v1)
- **Kreitmeir & Raschky, "The Heterogeneous Productivity Effects of Generative AI"** [WP 2024; supersedes the above]. **>36,000 GitHub users** in Italy and other European countries; DiD with user/day FE and event study. Findings: among affected Italian users, a **short-term increase in output quantity and quality for less experienced users** and a **decrease in productivity on more routine tasks for experienced users** (i.e., the ban's effect is heterogeneous by experience). — [arXiv 2403.01964](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01964v1); [Monash SoDa Labs project page](https://www.monash.edu/business/impact-labs/soda-labs/our-projects/the-heterogeneous-productivity-effects-of-generative-ai)
- **Quispe & Grijalba, "Impact of the Availability of ChatGPT on Software Development: A Synthetic Difference in Differences Estimation using GitHub Data"** [WP, Jun 2024]. Country-level **GitHub Innovation Graph** data (git pushes, repos, unique developers per 100k people); government ChatGPT bans as natural experiments; DiD, synthetic control, SDID. Findings: SDID shows **positive** effects on pushes, repos and developers; plain DiD suggests a negative effect, i.e. **results are estimator-dependent**. — [arXiv 2406.11046](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11046)

**(e) Agentic tools: descriptive / observational PR studies (non-causal, but key data infrastructure)**
- **Li, Zhang & Hassan, "The Rise of AI Teammates in Software Engineering (SE) 3.0" — AIDev dataset** [WP, 20 Jul 2025]. **>456,000 agent-authored PRs** (OpenAI Codex, Devin, GitHub Copilot agent, Cursor, Claude Code) across **~61,000 repos and ~47,000 developers**, identified via bot author names, branch prefixes, co-authorship messages, plus a same-repo human baseline. Findings: agents are fast but **agent PRs are accepted less often than human PRs**; accepted Codex PRs reviewed far faster (median 0.3 h vs 3.9 h for humans). Dataset public. — [arXiv 2507.15003](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.15003)
- **"A Task-Level Evaluation of AI Agents in Open-Source Projects"** [WP, Feb 2026] on AIDev-pop (>33k PRs, repos >100 stars): merged/submitted ratio **Codex 0.83, Cursor 0.67, Claude 0.66, Copilot 0.45**; a commit-quality analysis reports Claude highest "good commit" rate (0.68) and Codex lowest (0.32) despite highest acceptance. — [arXiv 2602.02345](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02345v1)
- **"Where Do AI Coding Agents Fail? An Empirical Study of Failed Agentic Pull Requests in GitHub"** [MSR '26 per search snippet]: docs, CI and build-update agent PRs merge at higher rates; **performance and bug-fix PRs have lowest acceptance**. — [arXiv 2601.15195](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.15195v1)
- **Khosravani & Mockus, "Detecting AI Coding Agents in Open Source: A Validated Multi-Method Census of 180 Million Repositories"** [WP, 23 Jun 2026], using **World of Code** snapshots. Detection taxonomy: bot account, commit-message/co-author trailer, author-name suffix (e.g. "(aider)"), config-file-only (CLAUDE.md, AGENTS.md, .cursorrules). Findings: Claude Code bot-account lookup finds 28,154 commits but multi-method union finds **850,157 (≈30× gap)**; Copilot SWE agent 1.13M commits in 85,739 projects; Codex leaves almost no commit trace (843 commits) despite 814,522 AIDev PRs; **>320,000 agent-attributed commits/month at peak (Dec 2025–Mar 2026)**; 315,426 projects with ≥1 AI-attributed commit; in studied projects **AI-attributed commits rose from 1.6% to 6.7% of non-bot activity (Dec 2025 → Mar 2026)**; adoption is bimodal ("born-with-AI" vs adopted years into development; Aider mostly in established projects). Limitations: recall unknown (lower bound), config files are proxies, ~4.8% of trailer detections were human commits. — [arXiv 2606.24429](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.24429v1)

**(f) Theory/model paper with circumstantial data**
- **Koren, Békés, Hinz & Lohmann, "Vibe Coding Kills Open Source"** [WP; arXiv Jan 2026; CEPR DP 21145]. Economic model: when AI agents assemble OSS packages, users stop reading docs, filing bugs, engaging maintainers; under traditional OSS business models widespread vibe coding **lowers OSS provision and welfare even as software output grows**, reducing variety and average quality of packages. Evidence is illustrative (Tailwind CSS: npm downloads rising while Stack Overflow questions fall); author told *The Register* evidence is mostly circumstantial. — [arXiv 2601.15494](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.15494); [CEPR DP listing](https://ideas.repec.org/p/cpr/ceprdp/21145.html); [The Register interview](https://www.theregister.com/2026/01/26/vibe_coding_hazardous_open_source/)

### Inferences
- Identification templates directly reusable for a mature-project panel: (1) **language-support contrast** (Python/JS/C supported early vs R/Haskell/Fortran not) à la Yeverechyahu; (2) **staggered DiD with Borusyak or Callaway–Sant'Anna estimators** keyed to repo-level adoption signals (config files, `Co-authored-by` trailers, bot PR authors); (3) **access shocks** (Italy's spring-2023 ban; China/Russia restrictions) for contributor-level geography. The ChatGPT launch (Nov 30, 2022) alone is a common shock with no clean control group; papers that use it pair it with an unaffected comparator.
- The convergent quantitative picture: per-developer output effects of single-digit percent (Song +2.1% individual; Daniotti +3.6%) and project-level effects from +6% (Song) to +28–40% (Yeverechyahu), with agent-era first-month spikes much larger (He et al. +281% lines in month 1) but fading. A long-history panel of mature projects should expect small and possibly transient level shifts, which argues for event-study plots rather than a single pre/post mean.
- Detection is the binding constraint: Khosravani & Mockus show single-signal detection undercounts by ~30× for Claude Code and Codex leaves almost no commit trace, so any "AI share" measure on mature repos will be a lower bound, and squash-merge practices in mature projects may erase trailers.
- Mature projects are likely to be "IDE-first"-like (contributors used Copilot/ChatGPT long before agents), so Agarwal et al.'s diminishing-returns result predicts smaller agent-era velocity shocks there than in greenfield repos.

### Gaps
- Exact sample sizes for Hoffmann et al. (number of maintainers near threshold) and Yeverechyahu et al. (number of packages/repos) were not retrieved (PDFs could not be parsed in this session).
- Publication status of He et al. (Cursor DiD) and Song et al. (ISR) could not be confirmed from the primary pages.
- A widely cited Claude Code PR study (Watanabe et al., "On the Use of Agentic Coding: An Empirical Study of Pull Requests on GitHub", arXiv ~2509.14745) surfaced only by name; its acceptance figures were not verified.
- No study found that estimates effects on **releases/release cadence**, **issue resolution time**, or **bus factor** in foundational projects.

## Q2. What did RCTs and field experiments find (METR 2025 + 2026 follow-up; Microsoft/Accenture/Fortune-100; Peng et al. 2023; others)?

### Takeaway
Lab and enterprise RCTs on well-scoped or corporate tasks show **speedups of ~20–56%** (Peng et al. 55.8% on a toy task; Cui et al. +26% completed tasks; Google ~21%), with gains concentrated among **less-experienced developers**. The only RCT on **experienced developers working in their own mature OSS repos** (METR, early 2025) found a **19% slowdown** despite developers believing they were 20% faster. METR's late-2025 follow-up points toward speedups (−18% / −4% time) but with confidence intervals spanning zero and severe selection problems; METR itself calls the estimate a poor proxy and is redesigning the study.

### Cited Findings
- **METR: Becker, Rush, Barnes & Rein, "Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity"** [WP; arXiv v1 12 Jul 2025, v2 25 Jul 2025; 51 pp.]. RCT: **16 experienced developers, 246 real tasks (issues) from their own mature repos**, each task randomly assigned AI-allowed or AI-disallowed; developers averaged **~5 years of prior experience on their repos**; tools mainly **Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet**; Feb–Jun 2025. Result: **AI increased completion time by 19%** (95% CI +2% to +39%). Developers forecast −24% beforehand and still believed afterwards AI had cut time ~20%; economics and ML experts forecast −39% and −38%. — [arXiv 2507.09089](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089); [METR Feb 2026 update (CI)](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)
  - Repo characteristics (per METR's July 2025 blog/paper body; not re-fetched this session): repositories averaged 22k+ stars and 1M+ lines of code. — [METR blog, Jul 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/)
- **METR follow-up, "We are Changing our Developer Productivity Experiment Design"** [blog/report, 24 Feb 2026]. Late-2025 study started Aug 2025: **57 developers (10 returning, 47 new; median 10 yrs experience), 143 repos, 800+ tasks**, new recruits from more diverse, smaller, greenfield and less mature repos; pay cut from $150/h to $50/h. Estimates (negative = speedup): **returning devs −18% (95% CI −38% to +9%)**; **new devs −4% (−15% to +9%)**. Selection problems: developers increasingly refused to work without AI; **30–50% of surveyed developers skipped tasks they didn't want to do without AI**; agent users multitasked while waiting, corrupting time tracking; differential attrition. METR: the estimate is "likely a bad proxy" for true impact, probably a lower bound on speedup, and only very weak evidence of change over time; options under consideration include developer-level randomization, shorter high-pay "intensive" experiments, and **observational data on AI use in OSS (commit stats, transcripts)**. Note: some secondary sites misread −18% as a slowdown. — [METR, 24 Feb 2026](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/); [Andrew Wegner commentary on sign confusion](https://andrewwegner.com/metr-ai-productivity-study-update.html)
- **Cui, Demirer, Jaffe, Musolff, Peng & Salz, "The Effects of Generative AI on High-Skilled Work: Evidence from Three Field Experiments with Software Developers"** [PR: *Management Science*, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2025.00535; SSRN Aug 2025]. Randomized Copilot access at **Microsoft, Accenture and an anonymous Fortune-100 electronics manufacturer**, **4,867 developers** pooled (earlier draft: 5,114). Result: **+26.08% (SE 10.3%) completed tasks** (pooled; individual experiments noisy). **Less-experienced developers adopted more and gained more.** Enterprise (not OSS) setting. — [SSRN 4945566](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4945566); [Management Science DOI](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.2025.00535)
- **Peng, Kalliamvakou, Cihon & Demirer, "The Impact of AI on Developer Productivity: Evidence from GitHub Copilot"** [WP, Feb 2023]. Controlled experiment with freelance developers asked to implement an HTTP server in JavaScript; **Copilot group completed the task 55.8% faster**; larger benefits for less experienced and older developers (not re-fetched this session). — [arXiv 2302.06590](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06590)
- **Paradis et al. (Google), "How much does AI impact development speed? An enterprise-based randomized controlled trial"** [ICSE SEIP 2025]. ~96 Google engineers on an enterprise-grade task; AI features associated with **~21% faster** completion with a wide CI (not re-fetched this session). — [arXiv 2410.12944](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12944)
- **Observational enterprise follow-ups (agentic era)**: a 2026 study of **Claude Code and Copilot CLI rollouts at Microsoft** reports a **~11.4% merged-PR lift** for Claude Code "any use" adopters (observational, enterprise, not OSS). — [arXiv 2607.01418](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.01418)

### Inferences
- The RCT literature splits by **familiarity × task scope**: speedups appear on unfamiliar, well-specified tasks and among juniors; the slowdown appears for experts on large, mature codebases with high implicit quality standards. This is exactly the population a foundational-project panel (matplotlib, PostgreSQL, curl, SQLite, FFmpeg) is about, so METR is the most relevant prior and its result argues against assuming large productivity effects in mature projects, at least through early 2025.
- METR's own pivot toward observational OSS data (commit statistics, transcripts) is a direct signal that repository-mining designs like little-helper's are now seen as complementary to RCTs, partly because RCTs have become infeasible as developers refuse the control arm.
- The METR selection problem (devs skipping tasks they won't do without AI) means mined task mix itself may shift after AI adoption; outcome metrics should track *composition* (docs vs features vs bug fixes) not just volume.

### Gaps
- No published RCT replication on mature OSS repos beyond METR's own late-2025 data was found as of Nov 2026; METR's redesigned study had not reported results.
- METR's late-2025 per-repo breakdown (mature vs greenfield) was not reported in the blog.

## Q3. What does research say about code quality, churn, duplication, maintainability and security of AI-assisted code?

### Takeaway
Two strands converge on **elevated technical debt**: causal repo-level DiD studies find **persistent increases in static-analysis warnings (+18% to +30%) and cognitive complexity (+11% to +41%)** after Cursor/agent adoption, and industry line-level data (GitClear, vendor) show **declining refactoring ("moved" code) and rising copy/paste duplication** since 2021. Security studies of Copilot output find **~25–40% of generated snippets/programs contain CWEs**, while studies of agent PRs in the wild show **security-related agent PRs are merged less and reviewed far more slowly**. Descriptive in-the-wild evidence on whether AI commits introduce more defects is mixed (some revert/SZZ proxies look *better* for AI commits).

### Cited Findings
- **Repo-level causal quality effects**: Cursor adoption → **static-analysis warnings +29.7%**, **cognitive complexity +40.7%** (or 25.1% per discussion section), both persistent; duplication n.s.; quality increases statistically account for much of the later velocity decay. — [He et al., arXiv 2511.04427](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.04427v2)
- Agent adoption (AIDev repos) → **warnings ~+18%, cognitive complexity ~+39%**, sustained after velocity gains fade. — [Agarwal, He & Vasilescu, arXiv 2601.13597](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.13597)
- Agent adoption in established projects → **Python cognitive complexity +~11%, cyclomatic +3–4%** (smaller than prior estimates). — [Xu, Cui, Ye & Zhou, arXiv 2607.01810](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.01810)
- Post-Copilot OSS code requires **more rework** to meet repository standards; core devs review 6.5% more and write 19% less original code. — [Xu et al. (Tilburg), arXiv 2510.10165](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.10165)
- **GitClear [VEN] "AI Copilot Code Quality" (2025)**: 211M changed lines 2020–2024; **moved (refactored) code fell from 24.8% of changed lines (2021) to 9.5% (2024)**; **copy/pasted lines rose from 8.4% to 12.3%**; 2024 the first year copy/paste exceeded moved lines; **~8× increase in frequency of duplicated blocks of ≥5 lines in 2024**. Not causal (trend coinciding with AI diffusion), proprietary methodology, mix of private and OSS repos. A June 2026 GitClear "Maintainability Gap" report (623M changes, 2023–2026) reportedly shows moved code at 3.8% in 2026 (single secondary source). — [DevClass summary of GitClear 2025](https://www.devclass.com/ai-ml/2025/02/20/ai-is-eroding-code-quality-states-new-in-depth-report/1626250); [LeadDev](https://leaddev.com/technical-direction/how-ai-generated-code-accelerates-technical-debt); [GitClear 2026 report page](https://gitclear.com/the_ai_code_quality_maintainability_gap)
- **Pearce et al., "Asleep at the Keyboard? Assessing the Security of GitHub Copilot's Code Contributions"** [PR: IEEE S&P 2022]: Copilot prompted in **89 CWE-relevant scenarios producing 1,689 programs; ~40% vulnerable** (not re-fetched this session). — [arXiv 2108.09293](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.09293)
- **Perry et al., "Do Users Write More Insecure Code with AI Assistants?"** [PR: ACM CCS 2023]: user study (~47 participants); those with an AI assistant wrote **significantly less secure code** yet were **more likely to believe it was secure** (not re-fetched this session). — [arXiv 2211.03622](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.03622)
- **Fu, Liang, Tahir et al., "Security Weaknesses of Copilot-Generated Code in GitHub Projects: An Empirical Study"** [PR: accepted *ACM TOSEM* 2025]. In-the-wild snippets self-identified as AI-generated in GitHub projects. Early version: **35.8% of 435 Copilot snippets contained CWEs**; TOSEM version: **733 snippets** from Copilot, CodeWhisperer, Codeium; **29.5% of Python and 24.2% of JavaScript snippets** had security weaknesses; Copilot Chat fixed **up to 55.5%** of issues when given static-analysis warnings. — [arXiv 2310.02059v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.02059v4)
- **Siddiq, Zhao, Lopes, Casey & Santos (Notre Dame), "Security in the Age of AI Teammates: An Empirical Study of Agentic Pull Requests on GitHub"** [*Information and Software Technology* per page header; arXiv 1 Jan 2026]. AIDev subset: **33,596 agent PRs in 2,807 repos (≥100 stars)**; **1,293 (3.85%) security-related**; Claude Code had the highest security share (14.6%), Codex lowest (1.3%); **security PRs merged 61.5% vs 77.3% non-security**; **median review latency 3.92 h vs 0.11 h**; agents mostly do supportive hardening (tests, docs, config, error handling) rather than targeted vuln fixes; rejection predicted by PR size and description length. Descriptive only. — [arXiv 2601.00477](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.00477v1)
- **In-the-wild defect proxies (descriptive)**: across World of Code, AI-attributed commits have higher bug-fix-keyword rates (Claude Code 1.32×, OpenHands 1.34×, Aider 1.26× vs human commits in same projects); revert rates lower for Claude Code (1.1% vs 1.6%) and Aider, higher for OpenHands; an adapted SZZ defect-introduction ratio of **0.33×–0.56×** (AI commits less often precede human bug fixes; authors call this the weakest evidence); **18 of 20 Claude Code projects showed higher bug-fix density after adoption** (+9.9 pp median). — [Khosravani & Mockus, arXiv 2606.24429](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.24429v1)
- **Retrospective LLM-contribution replay on mature C projects**: testing LLMs on real historic commits from **8 C projects including FFmpeg, Bison and wolfSSL**, LLMs work on small files/functions, feature improvements and tasks similar to prior ones, but **struggle with larger contexts, bug fixes and novel problems, often producing patches that fail silently** [EASE 2026]. — [arXiv 2604.23340](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.23340v1)
- **Counter-signal**: Google's 2024 DORA report [VEN] claimed AI adoption associated with +3.4% code quality (survey-based). — [DevClass summary](https://www.devclass.com/ai-ml/2025/02/20/ai-is-eroding-code-quality-states-new-in-depth-report/1626250)

### Inferences
- For mature projects, outcome metrics with established literature precedent are: SonarQube-style warnings, cognitive/cyclomatic complexity, duplicate-line density, moved-vs-copied lines (GitClear-style), revert rate, SZZ-style bug-introduction, and PR review latency. Using these keeps little-helper comparable with He et al. / Agarwal et al. / Xu et al.
- The complexity effect is shrinking across successive papers (≈+41% Cursor 2024–25 → ≈+39% agents → ≈+11% in established projects), consistent with either stronger review norms in established projects or better models; a mature-project panel can test which.
- Security evidence is mostly from generated snippets/benchmarks, not from merged code in critical infrastructure; mature projects with strong review (PostgreSQL, SQLite, curl) are a natural setting to test whether review gates neutralize the higher defect rates seen in lab studies.

### Gaps
- No causal study of security outcomes (CVE rates, OSS-Fuzz findings, security advisories) in mature projects before vs after LLMs was found.
- GitClear's repo sample composition (share OSS, which projects) is not public; findings can't be mapped onto foundational projects.
- No study quantifies "code churn" (lines rewritten within 2–4 weeks) causally for OSS; GitClear's churn claims are vendor-only.

## Q4. What is known about knowledge-commons effects: Stack Overflow decline, documentation, issue trackers, newcomer onboarding?

### Takeaway
There is strong quasi-experimental evidence that ChatGPT **reduced public Q&A on Stack Overflow by ≥25% within six months**, concentrated among newer users and popular languages, with no comparable decline on socially-tied communities (Reddit). Evidence on **newcomer onboarding to OSS** is newer and so far reassuring at the project level (no crowd-out of newcomers after agent adoption), but there is almost no causal work on **documentation, issue-tracker activity, or bug-report flows** in OSS, which remain theorized (e.g. "vibe coding" model) rather than measured.

### Cited Findings
- **del Rio-Chanona, Laurentsyeva & Wachs, "Large language models reduce public knowledge sharing on online Q&A platforms"** [PR: *PNAS Nexus* 2024, 3(9), pgae400]. DiD vs Russian and Chinese Q&A counterparts (where ChatGPT access is restricted) and similar math forums: **Stack Overflow activity fell 25% relative to counterparts within six months** of ChatGPT's release (preprint: ~16% initially, rising to ~25%); **post quality unchanged** (both high- and low-quality posts displaced); **steeper declines in Python and JavaScript**; authors treat 25% as a lower bound. — [PNAS Nexus PDF (repository copy)](https://real.mtak.hu/205789/1/pgae400.pdf); [UCL repository PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10202775/1/Large%20language%20models%20reduce%20public%20knowledge%20sharing%20on%20online%20Q%26A%20platforms.pdf); [arXiv 2307.07367](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.07367.pdf); [KRTK summary](https://krtk.hun-ren.hu/en/2024/10/the-impact-of-large-language-models-on-knowledge-sharing-a-25-decline-in-stack-overflow-activity/)
- **Burtch, Lee & Chen, "The consequences of generative AI for online knowledge communities"** [PR: *Scientific Reports* 14:10413, May 2024]. Stack Overflow vs Reddit developer communities, Oct 2021–Mar 2023: **declines in SO visits and question volume, sharpest on topics where ChatGPT performs well**; **no decline on Reddit** (social fabric as buffer); SO decline **concentrated among newer users**. — [Scientific Reports / PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11074245/); [Nature PDF](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-61221-0.pdf)
- A 2025 arXiv study confirms an **acceleration of decline in SO user contributions** after ChatGPT, but finds questions and answers became **longer and more difficult**. — [arXiv 2509.05879](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.05879)
- **Kabir et al., "Is Stack Overflow Obsolete? An Empirical Study of the Characteristics of ChatGPT Answers to Stack Overflow Questions"** [PR: CHI 2024]: **52% of ChatGPT answers to 517 SO questions contained incorrect information** (not re-fetched this session). — [arXiv 2308.02312](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.02312)
- Note: SO's question volume was already declining slowly from ~mid-2020; ChatGPT accelerated it; by May 2025 monthly questions had fallen to roughly 2009 levels (secondary sources). — [Pragmatic Engineer](https://blog.pragmaticengineer.com/stack-overflow-is-almost-dead); [dbreunig analysis](https://www.dbreunig.com/2025/05/16/stackoverflow-s-decline.md)
- **Newcomers / onboarding**:
  - **No crowd-out after agent adoption**: in established OSS projects, AI coding agent adoption produced **no significant decline in newcomer inflow, onboarding or retention**, and no decline in good-first-issue tasks (sparse measure). — [Xu, Cui, Ye & Zhou, arXiv 2607.01810](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.01810); [The New Stack coverage](https://thenewstack.io/ai-open-source-newcomers-study/)
  - **Good-first-issue longitudinal study** [dataset Nov 2025]: **43,906 newcomer PRs and 1,117 good-first-issue PRs across 37 popular GitHub repos, Jul 2021–Jun 2025**; newcomer PR descriptions grew longer coincident with genAI adoption, but **merge rates did not improve**; authors note causal claims are limited. — [Zenodo record 17638558](https://zenodo.org/records/17638558); [Moonlight review](https://www.themoonlight.io/en/review/a-longitudinal-analysis-of-good-first-issue-practices-and-newcomer-pull-requests-in-popular-oss-projects)
  - Copilot's benefits are **smaller for peripheral developers**, who also face larger increases in coordination time (lower project familiarity). — [Song, Agarwal & Wen, arXiv 2410.02091v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02091v2)
  - On Stack Overflow, the decline is concentrated among **newer users** — a potential pipeline effect for future OSS contributors. — [Burtch et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11074245/)
- **Self-admitted AI usage in OSS artifacts**: mining commits, issues and PRs for traces of ChatGPT/Copilot use yields a taxonomy of **64 usage tasks in 7 categories** (MSR 2024; not re-fetched this session). — [Tufano et al., arXiv 2402.16480](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.16480)
- **PR documentation**: GitHub's "Copilot for PRs" — **18,256 PRs** with partly AI-generated descriptions; quasi-experimental estimates of **19.3 hours shorter review time** and **1.57× higher merge likelihood**; developers often edit AI descriptions; early waitlisted adopters only [PR: FSE 2024, PACMSE 1(FSE)]. — [Xiao et al., arXiv 2402.08967](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.08967v1); [FSE 2024 program](https://2024.esec-fse.org/details/fse-2024-research-papers/105/Generative-AI-for-Pull-Request-Descriptions-Adoption-Impact-and-Developer-Interven)
- **Theory of user-engagement loss**: "vibe coding" reduces the documentation reads, bug reports and maintainer contact that sustain OSS; illustrated by Tailwind CSS (npm downloads up, SO questions down). — [Koren et al., arXiv 2601.15494](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.15494)
- **Foundation survey [VEN, brief]**: LF Research (Boysel & Lai, Oct 2026, with Red Hat, Futurewei, LF AI & Data, OpenSSF): **71% of contributors** say genAI makes them more willing to join unfamiliar projects; **64% of maintainers of large projects** say AI-assisted contributions lengthened review cycles; an estimated **25% of incoming PRs (31% in larger projects)** are low-quality AI submissions; 53% of maintainers disagree that genAI improved community trust. Survey only, no repository data. — [LF blog, 26 Oct 2026](https://www.linuxfoundation.org/blog/navigating-open-source-software-development-in-the-generative-ai-era-balancing-velocity-and-community-health); [LF report PDF](https://www.linuxfoundation.org/hubfs/Research%20Reports/Navigating-Open-Source-Software-Development-in-the-Generative-AI-Era.pdf)

### Inferences
- The knowledge-commons channel most likely to show up in mature-project GitHub history is **issue-tracker composition**: fewer "how do I" usage questions (substituted by LLMs, mirroring SO), possibly more low-quality AI-generated issues/PRs, and longer PR descriptions. Measuring issue volume by label/type and first-response latency before/after Nov 2022 would fill a clear gap.
- Because SO declines were steepest for Python and JavaScript, projects like matplotlib, NumPy, plotly and d3 are where substitution of community Q&A (including GitHub Discussions/issues used as support channels) should be most visible; C-heavy projects (PostgreSQL, FFmpeg, curl, SQLite) offer a within-sample contrast.
- Newcomer evidence at project level (no crowd-out) and individual level (peripheral devs benefit less; newer SO users exit) are not contradictory: inflow can be stable while newcomer *quality/retention trajectories* change; cohort-based retention curves would distinguish these.

### Gaps
- No causal study found on **documentation activity** (docs commits, docs-site traffic) in OSS after LLMs, other than Tailwind anecdotes.
- No causal study on **GitHub issue tracker volume/type** or **mailing-list activity** (important for PostgreSQL, FFmpeg, curl, which use mailing lists) after ChatGPT.
- No study of GitHub Discussions as a Stack Overflow substitute/complement.

## Q5. Are there studies specifically on mature/critical/infrastructure projects? What is known about heterogeneity by project age, size, language, core vs peripheral?

### Takeaway
**No published study targets foundational, decades-old infrastructure projects (e.g. PostgreSQL, FFmpeg, curl, SQLite, NumPy) with long pre/post panels.** The closest evidence is (i) METR's RCT on large mature repos (slowdown), (ii) Hoffmann et al.'s RD on top maintainers (shift from management to coding), (iii) the Peking University DiD on "established projects" (modest complexity increase, no newcomer crowd-out), and (iv) a retrospective LLM-replay study on mature C projects (FFmpeg, wolfSSL). Heterogeneity results consistently show **core vs peripheral** and **experience** splits, but they **point in opposite directions across studies**; heterogeneity by **project age, size or star count is essentially unreported**.

### Cited Findings
- **Mature-repo RCT**: METR's 2025 RCT used experienced devs on large mature repos they had worked on for ~5 years; AI **slowed them by 19%**. — [arXiv 2507.09089](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089). METR's late-2025 cohort deliberately added smaller, greenfield and less mature repos, but no maturity breakdown was reported. — [METR Feb 2026](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)
- **Top maintainers (critical people rather than critical projects)**: free Copilot access to GitHub's top maintainers shifts work **toward coding (+12%) and away from project management (−25%)**, with bigger effects for lower-ability maintainers. — [Hoffmann et al., CESifo WP 11479](https://www.ifo.de/DocDL/cesifo1_wp11479.pdf); [HBS Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/can-ai-help-managers-love-their-jobs-again)
- **Established projects**: agent adoption in established projects modestly raises complexity (+11% Python cognitive) without crowding out newcomers. — [arXiv 2607.01810](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.01810)
- **Mature C projects (retrospective replay, not field effect)**: LLMs struggle with large contexts and bug fixes in FFmpeg, Bison, wolfSSL and others. — [arXiv 2604.23340](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.23340v1)
- **Project activity level**: Copilot's skew toward incremental rather than substantive contributions is **larger in more active projects**. — [Yeverechyahu et al., arXiv 2409.08379](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.08379)
- **Prior AI exposure**: agent velocity gains are large in "agent-first" repos but minimal in repos with prior AI-IDE usage. — [Agarwal et al., arXiv 2601.13597](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.13597)
- **Project lifecycle at adoption**: agent adoption is bimodal: "born-with-AI" projects (e.g. ~68% of sampled OpenHands adopters) vs adoption years into development (Aider mainly in established projects); no popularity breakdown. — [Khosravani & Mockus, arXiv 2606.24429](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.24429v1)
- **Project size and review burden (survey)**: large projects (>50 contributors) report review pressure and maintainer burnout as the main concern; solo/small teams capture efficiency gains most readily. — [LF Research, Oct 2026](https://www.linuxfoundation.org/blog/navigating-open-source-software-development-in-the-generative-ai-era-balancing-velocity-and-community-health)
- **Core vs peripheral: conflicting directions**
  - Core developers gain *more*; peripheral gain less and pay more coordination time. — [Song, Agarwal & Wen](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02091v2)
  - Peripheral developers drive the productivity gain; core developers' own code output *falls 19%* as they review 6.5% more. — [Xu et al. (Tilburg)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.10165)
  - Experienced developers capture the productivity gains from AI-written Python, though newcomers use AI more. — [Daniotti et al., Science 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.08945v2); [Corvinus summary](https://www.uni-corvinus.hu/research/generative-ai-gives-seasoned-developers-the-edge/?lang=en)
  - In enterprises, less-experienced developers adopt more and gain more. — [Cui et al., Management Science](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4945566)
  - Italy ban: less experienced users' output quantity/quality rose short term; experienced users' routine-task productivity fell. — [Kreitmeir & Raschky 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01964v1)
  - Top maintainers with lower ability benefit more. — [Hoffmann et al.](https://www.ifo.de/DocDL/cesifo1_wp11479.pdf)
- **Language**: SO declines steeper for Python/JS; Cursor sample concentrated in JS/TS/Python; Yeverechyahu exploits Python vs R; Daniotti covers Python only. — [del Rio-Chanona et al.](https://real.mtak.hu/205789/1/pgae400.pdf); [He et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.04427v2)

### Inferences
- The sign of the core/peripheral effect seems to depend on the **outcome**: measured as *own code output*, core devs may lose (they absorb review burden; Xu et al.); measured as *project-level contributions*, core devs gain more (Song et al.). A mature-project study should separately track maintainers' authored commits, reviews/merges performed, and issue triage, rather than a single "activity" count.
- Foundational projects differ from AIDev/Cursor samples on precisely the dimensions likely to moderate effects: long histories, strict review norms, C/C++/Fortran share, mailing-list workflows (PostgreSQL, FFmpeg patch flows), squash merges and DCO/sign-off policies that hide AI trailers, and explicit AI policies (some bans). This makes little-helper's target sample a genuine gap rather than a replication.
- Within-language contrasts are available inside the target set: Python (matplotlib, NumPy, plotly.py) vs JS (d3, plotly.js) vs C (curl, SQLite, PostgreSQL, FFmpeg); Copilot/LLM capability was historically weaker in low-resource languages, which is the Yeverechyahu lever.

### Gaps
- No study found on Linux kernel, Apache Software Foundation, PostgreSQL, SQLite, curl, FFmpeg or NumPy specifically measuring before/after LLM changes in commits, contributors or releases (a targeted search for Linux kernel/Apache returned nothing).
- No study reports heterogeneity by project age, stars, or criticality scores (e.g. OpenSSF criticality score, Census II rankings).
- A student replication of maintainability effects across 199 JetBrains OSS repos exists only as a GitHub repo (unreviewed, results not seen). — [GitHub repo](https://github.com/baranakkanat/jetbrains-llm-maintainability)

## Q6. What is the economic-value framing that motivates why foundational projects matter?

### Takeaway
Hoffmann, Nagle & Zhou estimate OSS's **demand-side value at ~$8.8 trillion** (range $2.59–13.18T) versus a **supply-side recreation cost of ~$4.15 billion**, with value **highly concentrated**: ~96% of demand-side value comes from ~5% of developers and 84% from six languages. This concentration is the core argument for studying foundational projects and their small maintainer cores rather than the GitHub long tail.

### Cited Findings
- **Hoffmann, Nagle & Zhou, "The Value of Open Source Software"** [WP: HBS Strategy Unit WP 24-038, Jan 2024; SSRN 4693148]. Supply-side value (cost to recreate widely used OSS once): **$4.15 billion**; demand-side value (if each using firm had to build its own): **$8.8 trillion**, range **$2.59–13.18 trillion**, likely a lower bound; **top six languages = 84% of demand-side value**; **96% of demand-side value created by ~5% of developers**. — [SSRN 4693148](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4693148); [HBS Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/open-source-software-the-nine-trillion-resource-companies-take-for-granted); [range details (FOSS United)](https://forum.fossunited.org/t/papers-we-love-foss-edition/3150/2)
- The same team (Hoffmann, Nagle, plus GitHub/Microsoft co-authors) applies this "linchpin maintainer" lens to Copilot effects, motivating study of top maintainers specifically. — [Hoffmann et al., CESifo WP 11479](https://www.ifo.de/DocDL/cesifo1_wp11479.pdf)
- Daniotti et al. translate AI-assisted coding into economic value, estimating US AI-assisted coding value in the tens of billions annually (press coverage cites "almost $96bn"; figure not verified in primary text). — [The Stack coverage](https://www.thestack.technology/us-devs-most-likely-to-use-ai-to-write-code-as-machines-capture-almost-96bn-of-value/); [arXiv 2506.08945](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.08945v2)
- Koren et al. argue AI may raise software output while **lowering OSS provision and welfare** under traditional OSS business models (engagement-funded maintainership). — [arXiv 2601.15494](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.15494)

### Inferences
- Given that ~5% of developers produce ~96% of value, average treatment effects estimated on all of GitHub (Daniotti, Song, He) can be dominated by low-value repos; effects on the high-value core could be of different sign (cf. METR slowdown, Xu et al. core-developer output decline). This justifies little-helper's focus.
- The Koren model and Hoffmann et al.'s maintainer task-reallocation finding together generate a testable prediction for mature projects: **rising downstream usage with falling upstream engagement** (fewer issues/questions per download, more drive-by AI PRs, more maintainer coding but less community management).

### Gaps
- Publication status of "The Value of Open Source Software" beyond the HBS/SSRN working paper was not verified.
- No paper links AI-era changes in specific foundational projects to downstream economic value.

## Q7. Synthesis: consensus, disagreements, and gaps

### Takeaway
**Consensus**: AI coding tools raise measured OSS output modestly and often transiently; the gain is skewed toward maintenance/incremental work; quality and complexity costs are persistent; review/coordination burden rises; public Q&A (Stack Overflow) has declined substantially. **Disagreements**: who benefits (core vs peripheral, experienced vs novice), whether experienced developers on mature code are sped up at all (METR slowdown vs late-2025 tentative speedup), and the size of quality costs (complexity +11% to +41%). **Gaps**: foundational projects, long horizons (most panels end ≤2 years post-adoption), releases/issues/docs/security-in-production outcomes, non-GitHub workflows (mailing lists), and reliable AI-use detection.

### Cited Findings
- Output gains small and/or transient: +2.1% individual / +5.9% project (Song et al.); +3.6% quarterly output (Daniotti et al.); +28–40% contributions (Yeverechyahu et al.); Cursor lines-added spike fades after ~2 months (He et al.); minimal gains for repos with prior AI use (Agarwal et al.). — [Song](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02091v2); [Daniotti](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.08945v2); [Yeverechyahu](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.08379); [He](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.04427v2); [Agarwal](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.13597)
- Skew toward maintenance/incremental work and toward coding over project management. — [Yeverechyahu](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.08379); [Hoffmann et al.](https://www.ifo.de/DocDL/cesifo1_wp11479.pdf)
- Persistent quality debt: warnings +18% to +30%, complexity +11% to +41%. — [He](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.04427v2); [Agarwal](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.13597); [Xu/Zhou](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.01810)
- Rising coordination/review cost: integration coordination time +8% (Song); core devs review +6.5% while own output −19% (Xu/Tilburg); security agent PRs reviewed ~35× slower at the median (Siddiq et al.). — [Song](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02091v2); [Xu](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.10165); [Siddiq](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.00477v1)
- Expert slowdown vs tentative speedup: −19% speed (early 2025) vs −18%/−4% time with CIs spanning zero (late 2025). — [METR 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089); [METR 2026](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)
- Detection undercount: single-signal methods miss ~30× of Claude Code commits; Codex barely appears in commits. — [Khosravani & Mockus](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.24429v1)
- Knowledge commons: SO −25% within 6 months (lower bound), concentrated among new users. — [del Rio-Chanona et al.](https://real.mtak.hu/205789/1/pgae400.pdf); [Burtch et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11074245/)

### Inferences
- **Most valuable open contributions for little-helper**: (1) a long-horizon (inception → 2026) panel on foundational projects, enabling event studies around Copilot GA (mid-2022), ChatGPT (Nov 2022), and the agent wave (2025); (2) outcome breadth beyond commits/lines: release cadence, issue volume by type, time-to-first-response, PR review latency and acceptance, contributor inflow/retention cohorts, maintainer task mix (authoring vs reviewing vs triage), docs commits, revert/bug-fix rates; (3) in-sample contrasts by language (Python/JS vs C) and workflow (GitHub PRs vs mailing-list patches); (4) multi-signal AI-detection (trailers, bot accounts, config files, PR authorship), explicitly reported as lower bounds; (5) explicit project AI-policy dates (bans/disclosure rules) as additional treatment/moderator variables.
- Methodological lessons from existing work: use heterogeneity-robust staggered DiD (Borusyak; Callaway–Sant'Anna) rather than TWFE (He et al.'s TWFE complexity estimate failed pre-trends); expect contaminated controls (all projects partly treated after 2023), so effects are lower bounds; distinguish short-run spikes from long-run levels.
- Given almost-universal treatment after 2023, a pure before/after on mature projects risks confounding with secular trends (e.g. SO's pre-2022 decline, maintainer aging, funding changes); within-project contrasts (language-support timing, contributor geography for the Italy ban, contributor-level adoption via trailers) are the most credible available levers.

### Gaps
- No longitudinal study of **foundational infrastructure projects** specifically (confirmed absent in searches for Linux kernel/Apache/critical projects).
- No study measures effects on **releases**, **CVE/security advisories**, **mailing-list activity**, or **documentation** in OSS causally.
- Long-run (>2 years post-adoption) effects remain unmeasured; most agent-era papers cover ≤12–18 months.
- Publication statuses for several key 2025–26 preprints (He et al.; Agarwal et al.; Song et al. in ISR) not confirmed from primary pages; Hoffmann et al. and Yeverechyahu et al. sample sizes not retrieved.
- Benchmarks (SWE-bench), maintainer-burden incident reports (e.g. curl bug-bounty changes, "AI slop" accounts such as [arXiv 2607.04003](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.04003)), and vendor surveys were intentionally not covered in depth per scope.
