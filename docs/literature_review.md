# AI research has skipped open source's foundations

The existing evidence says AI coding tools raise open source output modestly and often only briefly. They tilt work toward incremental maintenance, leave lasting quality debt and push costs onto reviewers. Almost none of that evidence comes from the decades-old infrastructure projects little-helper targets, and that absence is the project's opening. About a dozen quasi-experimental studies find gains of **roughly +2% per developer to +28–40% per project**. Agent-era spikes, such as **a 281% jump in lines added in the first month after Cursor adoption**, fade within about two months, while **static-analysis warnings (+18–30%) and code complexity (+11–41%) stay elevated**. The only randomized trial of experienced developers in their own mature repositories found AI made them **19% slower**. Capability benchmarks climbed fast: Epoch AI's independent SWE-bench Verified runs rose **from 31% to 83.5% in 17 months** before Epoch declared the benchmark flawed. Those benchmarks, though, are built from about a dozen Python repositories (matplotlib among them), and maintainers would reject **roughly half of the patches that pass their tests**. Industry trackers count AI fingerprints across GitHub's long tail, where single-signal detection undercounts by as much as **30×**. No study, tracker or benchmark follows PostgreSQL, FFmpeg, matplotlib, plotly or d3 from inception to now, and none tracks performance across a long commit history. For little-helper this implies four commitments. First, treat local git clones as canonical and GitHub as one review venue among several, since PostgreSQL's GitHub repository is "just a *mirror*" and FFmpeg now reviews on mailing lists and its own Forgejo forge. Second, measure composition, review load and security alongside volume, because the clearest 2026 signal is AI-found bugs (**PostgreSQL CVEs per release went from 2–3 to 29**). Third, report AI attribution as a multi-signal lower bound. Fourth, identify effects from contrasts within and across projects rather than a single November 2022 before/after split. The later performance phase can reuse the GSO, SWE-fficiency and SWE-Perf pipelines and existing asv dashboards. It arrives just as AI's measured ability to speed up real repositories jumped **from about 27% to 88% on GSO in ten months**.

## A dozen causal studies find modest, fading gains and lasting debt

The academic literature is larger and more rigorous than its public reputation. Roughly twelve quasi-experimental papers now estimate AI effects on open source, and they rely on four identification levers. Copilot launched supporting some languages (Python) but not others (R); GitHub gave free Copilot to developers above a "top maintainer" cutoff, which permits a regression discontinuity; committed artifacts such as `.cursorrules`, `CLAUDE.md`, agent-authored PRs and `Co-authored-by: Claude` trailers reveal when a repository adopted a tool, enabling staggered difference-in-differences; and access shocks such as Italy's 2023 ChatGPT ban supply natural experiments. The table collects the studies most relevant to little-helper's design.

| Study | Identification | Sample | Headline effect |
|---|---|---|---|

| [Hoffmann et al.](https://www.ifo.de/DocDL/cesifo1_wp11479.pdf) | Regression discontinuity on free Copilot for top maintainers | "Linchpin" maintainers | Coding **+12%**, project management **−25%** ([HBS](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/can-ai-help-managers-love-their-jobs-again)) |
| [Song, Agarwal & Wen](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02091v2) | Staggered adoption from GitHub's proprietary usage data, generalized synthetic control | GitHub, 2021–22 | Project output **+5.9%**, individual **+2.1%**, integration coordination time **+8%** |
| [Xu et al. (Tilburg)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.10165) | DiD after Copilot introduction | OSS developers | Core developers review **6.5% more** code while their own original output falls **19%** |

### Gains arrive as spikes and fade where AI was already in use

Read together, the estimates agree on the shape of the effect more than its size. **Per-developer effects sit in the single digits** (Song's +2.1%, Daniotti's +3.6%), project-level effects are larger, and agent-era effects arrive as spikes that decay rather than as lasting level shifts. Agarwal, He and Vasilescu add the result that matters most for little-helper. Repositories whose contributors already used AI in their editors saw minimal or short-lived gains when agents arrived ([Agarwal et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.13597)). Foundational projects, whose maintainers have had Copilot since 2022, resemble those "IDE-first" repositories. The literature therefore predicts small agent-era shocks exactly where little-helper looks, and the spike-then-decay shape argues for event-study plots over single pre/post means.

Quality costs are the most consistent result, and their estimated size shrinks across successive papers. Complexity rose roughly 41% for Cursor adopters, 39% for agent adopters, and 11% in established projects ([Xu, Cui, Ye & Zhou](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.01810)). Either stronger review norms in established projects or better models could explain the decline, and a panel of mature projects can tell the two apart.

Vendor line-level data points the same way without being causal. GitClear reports **"moved" (refactored) code falling from 24.8% of changed lines in 2021 to 9.5% in 2024** ([DevClass on GitClear](https://www.devclass.com/ai-ml/2025/02/20/ai-is-eroding-code-quality-states-new-in-depth-report/1626250)). It put that share at **3.8%** in 2026, with changes touching code older than 12 months down **74%** ([GitClear](http://www.gitclear.com/the_ai_code_quality_maintainability_gap)).

Security studies compound the concern. Copilot output was vulnerable in about **40%** of CWE-relevant scenarios ([Pearce et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.09293)); weaknesses appeared in **29.5% of Python and 24.2% of JavaScript** AI snippets found in real GitHub projects ([Fu et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.02059v4)); and security-related agent PRs merge at **61.5% versus 77.3%** for other agent PRs, with median review latency of **3.92 versus 0.11 hours** ([Siddiq et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.00477v1)). The one study set in little-helper's own territory replayed historic commits from mature C projects, including FFmpeg and wolfSSL. LLMs handled small, familiar changes but struggled with large contexts and bug fixes, often producing patches that "fail silently" ([EASE 2026 replay study](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.23340v1)).

### Trials split on familiarity, and benefits split on what gets counted

The randomized trials divide along familiarity and task scope. METR's 16 developers averaged about five years on their own repositories. They forecast a 24% speedup, experienced a 19% slowdown, and still believed afterward that AI had saved them about 20% ([METR RCT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089)). Enterprise and toy-task trials find speedups instead: **+26% completed tasks** across Microsoft, Accenture and a Fortune-100 firm ([Cui et al.](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4945566)), and 55.8% faster completion of a JavaScript HTTP-server task ([Peng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06590)). In both, gains concentrate among less-experienced developers.

METR's late-2025 follow-up pointed toward speedups: −18% time for returning developers (CI −38% to +9%) and −4% for new recruits (CI −15% to +9%). But it broke down on selection, because **30–50% of developers skipped tasks they would not do without AI**. METR now calls its own estimate "likely a bad proxy." Among its redesign options it lists **observational data on AI use in OSS**, such as commit statistics ([METR update](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)). That is a direct signal that repository mining like little-helper's is now seen as a necessary complement to trials. The selection problem also implies that AI changes *which* tasks get done, so the project should track the mix of work and not only its volume.

The literature disagrees about who benefits, and the disagreement is informative. [Song et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02091v2) find core developers gain more while peripheral developers pay more coordination time; [Xu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.10165) find peripheral developers drive the gains while core developers' own code output falls 19% as they absorb review; Daniotti et al. find experienced developers capture the gains; and Cui et al. find juniors gain more in enterprises. The sign appears to depend on the outcome. Measured as *own authored code*, core maintainers can lose; measured as *project-level contributions*, they gain. Hoffmann et al.'s maintainers shifted from project management toward coding. A study of foundational projects should therefore count three things separately for maintainers: authored commits, reviews and merges performed, and triage. A single activity count hides exactly these shifts.

### Commons effects are measured on Stack Overflow, not inside projects

Knowledge-commons evidence is strongest outside the projects themselves. **Stack Overflow activity fell 25% relative to counterparts within six months of ChatGPT**, with steeper declines in Python and JavaScript ([del Rio-Chanona et al., *PNAS Nexus*](https://real.mtak.hu/205789/1/pgae400.pdf)). The decline concentrated among newer users, while Reddit's socially tied communities held steady ([Burtch et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11074245/)).

Inside OSS, the newcomer evidence is mixed. Agent adoption caused no newcomer crowd-out at the project level ([Xu, Cui, Ye & Zhou](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.01810)). But a 37-repository longitudinal dataset found newcomer PR descriptions getting longer while **merge rates did not improve** ([good-first-issue dataset](https://zenodo.org/records/17638558)). The "Vibe Coding Kills Open Source" model predicts AI raises downstream use while eroding the bug reports, documentation reads and maintainer contact that sustain projects ([Koren et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.15494)). Maintainers perceive **25% of incoming PRs (31% in large projects)** as low-quality AI submissions ([LF Research](https://www.linuxfoundation.org/blog/navigating-open-source-software-development-in-the-generative-ai-era-balancing-velocity-and-community-health)), though that is a perception, not a measurement.

The economic case for little-helper's focus is concentration. Open source's demand-side value is estimated at **$8.8 trillion**, with **~96% created by ~5% of developers** ([Hoffmann, Nagle & Zhou](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4693148)). Average effects estimated across all of GitHub are therefore dominated by low-value repositories, and the effect on the high-value core could differ even in sign.

## Benchmarks saturated on a dozen repos while trackers counted the long tail

### Capability benchmarks drew on little-helper's repos, then saturated

Capability organizations measure what AI can do on frozen snapshots of real repositories, and several of those repositories are on little-helper's list. SWE-bench draws 2,294 tasks from 12 Python repositories, **184 of them from matplotlib** ([HF SWE-bench](https://huggingface.co/datasets/princeton-nlp/SWE-bench)), which also supplies 34 of the 500 Verified tasks ([HF Verified](https://huggingface.co/datasets/princeton-nlp/SWE-bench_Verified)) and **102 of the 1,888 tasks** in the continuously updated SWE-bench-Live ([HF SWE-bench-Live](https://huggingface.co/datasets/SWE-bench-Live/SWE-bench-Live)). SWE-bench Multimodal covers Chart.js and openlayers but **neither d3 nor plotly** ([HF Multimodal](https://huggingface.co/datasets/princeton-nlp/SWE-bench_Multimodal)).

Epoch's own runs took Verified from 31% (GPT-4o, Nov 2024) to 83.5% (Claude Opus 4.7, Apr 2026) ([Epoch data](https://epoch.ai/data/benchmark_data.zip)). Vals AI recorded 97% and archived the benchmark as saturated ([Vals](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/swebench)). On Sep 3, 2026 Epoch rated it **"Flawed."** An OpenAI audit had found broken tests in 59.4% of the tasks it reviewed, which implies at least 16.4% of all tasks are broken. It also found every frontier model had seen some of the problems in training ([Epoch review](https://epoch.ai/benchmarks/swe-bench-verified/review)).

Memorization is measurable directly. From the issue text alone, models name the buggy file **76% of the time on SWE-bench repositories versus about 53% elsewhere** ([SWE-Bench Illusion](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.12286)). AI agents should therefore look unusually capable on matplotlib, because labs have trained and evaluated against it. That makes benchmark exposure a measurable moderator rather than noise.

On longer tasks, the frontier outran its instruments. METR's 50% time horizon doubled every **89–131 days** depending on the window ([METR TH1.1](https://metr.org/blog/2026-01-29-time-horizon-1-1/)); its last point, about 17.4 hours, exceeded the suite's 16-hour reliability ceiling, and METR stopped maintaining the page on Sep 8, 2026 ([METR](https://metr.org/time-horizons/)). MirrorCode, a whole-program reimplementation benchmark, went from **4.4% to 97.8%** between Aug 2025 and Oct 2026 ([Epoch data](https://epoch.ai/data/benchmark_data.zip); [MirrorCode](https://epoch.ai/MirrorCode)). FrontierSWE's 20-hour tasks include **FFmpeg libswscale optimization and a PostgreSQL 18 task**, with the top model at 69.9% ([FrontierSWE](https://www.frontierswe.com/)), yet ProgramBench, which asks agents to rebuild FFmpeg and SQLite from their executables, had **no task fully solved** as of May 2026 ([ProgramBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.03546)). Benchmarks now saturate or are archived within 6–18 months, which raises the value of a reproducible observational panel. Epoch's CC BY 4.0 CSVs and METR's YAML give little-helper dated capability series to use as covariates ([Epoch Benchmarks](https://epoch.ai/benchmarks)).

### Maintainers would reject half of test-passing patches

Passing tests is not the same as being mergeable. METR had four active maintainers from scikit-learn, Sphinx and pytest blind-review **296 AI patches that had passed the grader**. The comparison baseline was human "golden" patches, which the maintainers accepted only about 68% of the time. After normalizing to that baseline, **maintainer merge rates ran 24.2 percentage points below grader scores, so roughly half of passing patches would not be merged**. The maintainer-judged 50% time horizon was **about 7× shorter** than the grader's ([METR](https://metr.org/notes/2026-03-10-many-swe-bench-passing-prs-would-not-be-merged-into-main/)).

Other evidence agrees. An earlier METR study found **0 of 15** agent PRs mergeable as-is ([METR Aug 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-08-12-research-update-towards-reconciling-slowdown-with-time-horizons/)). The strictest 2026 rubric benchmark, built by OSS developers from repositories they maintain, puts the top model at **47.3%** ([Cognition FrontierCode 2.0](https://cognition.com/blog/frontier-code-2.0)), though Cognition also sells coding agents.

GitHub reports agent-involved PRs merging within two weeks **90%** of the time, versus about 86% for others ([Octoverse 2026](https://github.blog/news-insights/octoverse/octoverse-commits-pushes-reach-new-highs-in-2026-as-developers-build-with-agents/)). That figure is dominated by self-merges: about **75% of Codex and Jules PRs sit in zero-star repositories**, and the median Codex PR merges in **0.5 minutes** ([arXiv 2604.00917](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.00917v1)). Merge rates mean something only where the PR author differs from the person who merges.

### Usage trackers count the long tail and miss most AI use

Usage trackers measure the long tail and say so. Octoverse 2026 reports that agents started **over 40% of all PRs in September 2026, up from 17% in March**, and that from June 2026 about **55% of commits came from brand-new repositories**; GitHub calls its agent signal a "floor" and restated earlier years on new definitions, so figures cannot be compared across editions ([Octoverse 2026](https://github.blog/news-insights/octoverse/octoverse-commits-pushes-reach-new-highs-in-2026-as-developers-build-with-agents/)). Commit-trailer counts put Claude Code at **0.7% of weekly public commits in Sep 2025 and 4.5% by Mar 2026** ([Kinlan](https://aifoc.us/damn-claude-thats-a-lot-of-commits/)), with about **90% of its output in repositories with fewer than two stars** ([WinBuzzer on claudescode.dev](https://winbuzzer.com/2026/03/26/claude-code-90-percent-output-low-star-github-repos-xcxwbn/)). A World of Code census found that combining detection methods surfaces **850,157 Claude Code commits where bot-account lookup finds 28,154**, and that AI-attributed commits in the projects studied rose from 1.6% to 6.7% of non-bot activity between Dec 2025 and Mar 2026 ([Khosravani & Mockus](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.24429v1)). Developers self-report that AI writes **42% of their committed code** ([Sonar](https://www.sonarsource.com/company/press-releases/sonar-data-reveals-critical-verification-gap-in-ai-coding/)), an order of magnitude above trailer-based shares, which gives a rough gauge of how much invisible use marker methods miss. No tracker publishes an AI-marker series restricted to foundational repositories.

### Security is where AI visibly reached foundational projects

Security is the one channel where AI capability has plainly reached foundational projects, and it shows up in their public history. Anthropic's Project Glasswing named a **16-year-old FFmpeg bug** that automated tools had exercised 5 million times without catching ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/glasswing)). Its May 2026 update reported 23,019 candidate findings across more than 1,000 OSS projects. Only **about 530 high/critical findings had been disclosed and 75 patched**, and Anthropic described maintainers as "severely capacity constrained" ([Glasswing update](https://www.anthropic.com/research/glasswing-initial-update)).

Two regimes appear in the record. The first was a slop flood: curl's confirmed-vulnerability rate fell from "north of 15%" to "below 5%," and the project ended its bug bounty on Jan 31, 2026 ([Stenberg](https://daniel.haxx.se/blog/2026/01/26/the-end-of-the-curl-bug-bounty/)). The second is a flood of real but often duplicate bugs: the Linux kernel's CVE fix rate "suddenly quadrupled" to about **33 CVEs per day**, and a batch of 79 bugs found with Anthropic's Mythos model yielded about 10 real fixes ([LWN](https://lwn.net/SubscriberLink/1096908/c6356cdf7f44d632/)).

PostgreSQL offers a ready-made template. One consultant's analysis of public git, release notes and list archives found **CVEs per release rising from 2–3 to 14 (May 2026) and 29 (Aug 2026)**, release-note items roughly doubling, and 26 stable-branch fix commits disclosing AI involvement, all since April 2026, while total list traffic did not change ([pgsql-hackers](https://www.postgresql.org/message-id/CACzdQB4Ojc0dC7-USnr5Ga8BA8WjZRswz%3D8cKAqCUPmrdzutWQ%40mail.gmail.com)). It is a single non-peer-reviewed mailing-list post, but its code is public, and its design is exactly what little-helper could generalize.

## No one has followed foundational projects through the agent era

Across all five strands of evidence, the gaps are specific and they line up. **No published study targets foundational, decades-old projects with inception-to-now panels**; targeted searches for the Linux kernel, the Apache projects, PostgreSQL, SQLite, curl, FFmpeg and NumPy returned nothing. No study reports heterogeneity by project age, stars or criticality score, and most agent-era panels end within 12–18 months of adoption. No causal study covers releases, documentation, issue-tracker composition, mailing-list activity or production security outcomes, and no tracker publishes AI-marker series for named critical projects. Benchmarks barely touch the target set, since none covers plotly or d3 and PostgreSQL appears in a single FrontierSWE task, and **no benchmark tracks how performance evolves across a long commit history**, because GSO, SWE-fficiency and SWE-Perf each score one before/after optimization.

Foundational projects are not a replication target, because they differ on exactly the moderators the literature flags: histories of 20 to 30-plus years, strict review norms, heavy C and assembly content, mailing-list workflows, squash-merge and sign-off conventions that can strip AI trailers, and explicit, dated AI policies. Those differences turn the literature's conflicting results into testable predictions.

| Prediction | Prior evidence | How little-helper can test it |

The strongest objection deserves a direct answer. Much AI use in foundational projects is invisible in git, because Copilot completions and ChatGPT copy-paste from 2022–2024 leave no trace. Several of the most consequential channels never enter repository history at all: the Stack Overflow decline, AI crawler load on self-hosted forges, and downstream usage that bypasses maintainers. And with a few dozen projects all exposed to the same shock, strong causal claims will be hard to earn.

The right positioning follows from that objection. Little-helper should be a **reproducible, timestamped measurement observatory first, and a source of pre-registered quasi-experimental estimates second**. Under that framing a careful null result counts as a finding: "foundational projects show no velocity break but a security-fix surge" is a publishable conclusion. The global Octoverse explosion and a quiet foundational core can both be true, and demonstrating that would itself be little-helper's contribution.

## Five design decisions turn the gap into evidence

### Select projects as matched contrasts, not a list of famous names

Project selection should be defensible enough to pre-register. The recipe starts by combining two independent importance signals: usage or dependency centrality, drawn from Census III ([LF](https://linuxfoundation.org/press/open-source-usage-trends-and-security-challenges-revealed-in-new-study)), deps.dev dependents, ecosyste.ms's 523-package PyPI critical set ([Nesbitt](https://nesbitt.io/2026/05/13/showing-our-work)) or Debian popcon for C and system tools ([popcon](https://popcon.debian.org/)), and a criticality ranking such as the OpenSSF Criticality Score ([Google OSS blog](https://opensource.googleblog.com/2020/12/finding-critical-open-source-projects.html?m=0)) or the LF list that corrects its false positives ([LF critical-projects](https://github.com/linuxfoundation/critical-projects)). It then filters on longevity, requiring a first commit before 2015 and at least 12 active months in 2019–2022 so pre-trends can be estimated, and stratifies by data regime, language, AI policy and benchmark exposure. Each list's version, retrieval date and score belongs in the record. Census III's CC BY-ND license means citing its rankings rather than republishing a modified table, and PyPI or npm download counts belong in selection only as ranks, never as outcomes.

Within that frame, the README's five projects work best as deliberate contrasts. matplotlib and plotly.py are two Python plotting libraries that differ sharply in benchmark exposure. plotly.js and d3 are unexposed JavaScript visualization libraries, with d3 analyzed as many `d3-*` module repositories mapped to one project. PostgreSQL, FFmpeg and curl contrast mailing-list workflows with a GitHub-native one, and projects with no formal AI policy (PostgreSQL, FFmpeg) with curl's report-screening rules ([melissawm list](https://github.com/melissawm/open-source-ai-contribution-policies)). Three additions would raise the payoff: NumPy or pandas, whose public asv histories and heavy GSO and SWE-fficiency coverage anchor the performance phase; CPython or LLVM, for their long-running performance trackers; and SQLite or the Linux kernel as closed-contribution and mailing-list reference points. The language split matters for exposure too. AI involvement in merged GitHub PRs runs **44.1% for TypeScript and 41.1% for Python versus 21.2% for Java** ([Octoverse 2026](https://github.blog/news-insights/octoverse/octoverse-commits-pushes-reach-new-highs-in-2026-as-developers-build-with-agents/)).

### Treat git as canonical and GitHub as one review venue

The README's "GitHub-hosted only" scope collides with how foundational projects actually work. PostgreSQL's GitHub repository says outright that it is "just a *mirror* - we don't work with pull requests on github" ([GitHub](https://github.com/postgres/postgres)). FFmpeg launched its own forge in 2025 ([FFmpeg news](https://ffmpeg.org/index.html)) and now takes changes "to Forgejo or the development mailing list" ([FFmpeg developer docs](https://ffmpeg.org/developer.html)). Commit history on a mirror is complete, but PR, issue and review data there is absent or meaningless. Each project therefore belongs to a **data regime**, and PR- or review-based outcomes should be compared only within one.

| FFmpeg | git.videolan.org / code.ffmpeg.org (canonical status unverified) | ffmpeg-devel and patchwork; Forgejo since mid-2025 | No | Forge launch; in-project LLM reviewer; Big Sleep reports in 2025 ([ItsFOSS](https://itsfoss.com/news/ffmpeg-google-fiasco/)) |
| curl | GitHub | GitHub PRs | Yes | Bug bounty ended Jan 31, 2026 |

| SQLite | Fossil | Closed to outside code ([SQLite](https://www.sqlite.org/copyright.html)) | No | None needed for contribution flows |
| Linux | kernel.org | LKML | No | `Assisted-by` tag adopted 2026, deprecated Oct 2026 ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/10/14/linux-kernel-devs-drop_obligatory_confession_of_ai_tool_usage/5303140)) |

The fix is to relabel the scope as "git-canonical, with review data from GitHub where GitHub is the review venue." Clone from the canonical upstream where one exists, record the mirror URL, and verify that SHAs match. Phase 1 can then stay GitHub-API-only for review data without misrepresenting the mirror projects. A mailing-list collector in a phase 1.5 would make PostgreSQL's and FFmpeg's review processes visible: GrimoireLab's Perceval reads mbox and pipermail archives ([GrimoireLab](https://chaoss.github.io/grimoirelab/)), and PaStA maps list patches to commits ([PaStA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.03147)). Migration dates for LLVM, CPython and FFmpeg need explicit terms in every model. Otherwise the 2023 and 2025 workflow switches will load onto "post-ChatGPT" coefficients.

The stack in the README fits this division of labor. Bulk commit history should come from `git log --numstat` piped into polars, which avoids rate limits entirely, leaving httpx and the REST API for PR, review, issue and release objects. The authenticated limit is **5,000 requests per hour** plus undisclosed secondary limits, so the client should honor `retry-after` and `x-ratelimit-reset` and log headers on every request, because "continuing to make requests while rate limited may result in your integration being banned" ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/rest/using-the-rest-api/rate-limits-for-the-rest-api)). The Events API returns only **30 days and 300 events** ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/rest/activity/events)), so historical event series must come from GH Archive ([gharchive.org](https://www.gharchive.org/)) or per-object timestamps. Software Heritage ([SWH Graph Dataset](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.07824)) and World of Code ([WoC](https://worldofcode.org/)) cover cross-forge commit graphs, while GHTorrent is defunct ([OpenHub](https://openhub.net/p/ghtorrent)) and its domain served an unrelated gambling site when checked on 2026-11-12, so it should not be used.

Git hygiene decides whether trends are real ([Flint et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.11339)). The pipeline should store author and committer dates with their timezone offsets, count PRs or `--first-parent` commits in squash-merge projects, strip bots before computing contributor metrics, apply `.mailmap` and identity merging, exclude vendored and generated paths, and start panels after the CVS and SVN import eras. Matching the README's "timestamp everything" means caching raw API responses as JSONL with `retrieved_at` and the query parameters, fixing the analysis window in config, archiving each analyzed snapshot through Software Heritage ([Save Code Now](https://archive.softwareheritage.org/save/)) with its SWHID recorded, pseudonymizing emails with a salted hash, and minting a Zenodo DOI per release. Open access is a condition, not an option: GitHub's Acceptable Use Policies permit research use of public data only if the resulting publications are open access ([GitHub AUP](https://docs.github.com/en/site-policy/acceptable-use-policies/github-acceptable-use-policies)).

### Measure composition, review load and security alongside volume

Raw volume is the weakest outcome. AI inflates lines: a median Claude PR changes 376 lines against about 60 for human PRs ([arXiv 2604.00917](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.00917v1)). Squash-merge policy also distorts commit counts. The core monthly panel should use CHAOSS names ([CHAOSS](https://chaoss.community/kb-metrics-and-metrics-models/)) for first-parent commits, distinct authors, newcomers with 6- and 12-month cohort retention, turnover, truck factor, commit concentration and churn net of vendored code. Two further measure sets keep little-helper comparable with prior work: static-analysis warnings and cognitive and cyclomatic complexity match the measures in He et al., Agarwal et al. and the PKU study, while GitClear-style measures need only git: moved versus copy-pasted lines, duplicated blocks, edits to code older than 12 months, two-week churn, revert rates and SZZ-style bug introduction.

Composition is the most valuable outcome family and the one the literature most lacks. Classifying commits and PRs into docs, tests, features, fixes, performance and security directly tests the incremental-versus-substantive shift. Counting each core maintainer's authored commits, reviews, merges and triage separately tests the review-burden hypothesis. GitHub-native projects add review outcomes: time to first non-bot response, review rounds, PR latency, external-PR share, and acceptance where author and merger differ. Every project, mirror or not, supports the PostgreSQL security template of security-fix commits and CVE references per release, release-note items, stable-branch churn, and credit lines naming AI finders. Issue-tracker composition, meaning the share of usage questions in matplotlib's and plotly's issues and Discussions, is where Stack Overflow substitution should appear, with the C projects as a contrast. AI-policy file commits, GitHub's PR-disable setting of Feb 13, 2026 ([changelog](https://github.blog/changelog/2026-02-13-new-repository-settings-for-configuring-pull-request-access/)) and its open-PR caps of Jun 17, 2026 ([changelog](https://github.blog/changelog/2026-06-17-limit-open-pull-requests-for-users-without-write-access/)) enter as dated governance covariates.

### Count AI fingerprints as a floor, never a census

AI attribution in git is a stack of signals with very different precision and coverage, and every count it produces is a lower bound. Single-signal methods undercount Claude Code by about 30×. Codex leaves almost no commit trace (**843 commits against 814,522 AIDev PRs**), and about 4.8% of trailer hits turn out to be human commits ([Khosravani & Mockus](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.24429v1)). Copilot inline completions leave **no trace at all** ([Kinlan](https://aifoc.us/damn-claude-thats-a-lot-of-commits/)).

| Signal | Example | Precision | Blind spots | Works on mailing-list projects? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bot author accounts | `copilot-swe-agent[bot]`, `devin-ai-integration[bot]`, `google-labs-jules[bot]` ([PR Arena](https://github.com/aavetis/PRarena)) | High | Agents committing as the human (Codex) | Rarely |
| Tool trailers and name suffixes | `Co-Authored-By: Claude <noreply@anthropic.com>`; "(aider)" ([aider](https://aider.chat/docs/git.html)); Copilot's `Agent-Logs-Url` since Mar 2026 ([changelog](https://github.blog/changelog/2026-03-20-trace-any-copilot-coding-agent-commit-to-its-session-logs)) | High, with a small false-positive rate | Disabled attribution, squash merges, rewritten messages | Only if committers keep trailers |
| PR-body watermarks | "Generated with Claude Code" | High | Edited descriptions | No |
| Branch prefixes | `copilot/`, `codex/`, `cursor/` | Medium | Humans reuse names | No |
| Agent config files | `AGENTS.md`, `CLAUDE.md`, `.cursorrules`, `.github/copilot-instructions.md` ([arXiv 2510.21413](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.21413v1)) | Intent-to-treat proxy | Individual private use | Yes |
| Project disclosure tags | `Assisted-by:`, `Generated-by:` | High where mandated | Conventions differ; Kubernetes forbids such trailers; the kernel deprecated its tag in Oct 2026 | Yes |
| AI-finder credit lines | Credits naming Codex Security, Big Sleep or Claude ([pgsql-hackers](https://www.postgresql.org/message-id/CACzdQB4Ojc0dC7-USnr5Ga8BA8WjZRswz%3D8cKAqCUPmrdzutWQ%40mail.gmail.com)) | High | Unstandardized phrasing | Yes |
| Content-based detectors | Classifiers over diffs | Low (best lab F1 ≈ 0.83, [Suh et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.04299v1)) | Unreliable per commit | Aggregate checks only |

Matching should key on email rather than display name, and every AI-policy and config file should be dated from git history and used as a treatment marker. Tool launches and default changes are breaks in the detection series itself: Claude Code's first observed commit (Feb 2025), Cursor's trailer change (Jan 2026) and the kernel's tag deprecation (Oct 2026) will masquerade as adoption shocks if left unmodeled. On mirror and mailing-list projects only commit-message trailers and tags survive, and whether committers preserve them is itself a project practice to record. Every attribution-based result should sit beside marker-free outcomes such as volume, PR size, duplication, newcomers and review latency, and content classifiers of the kind Daniotti et al. used belong only in aggregate sensitivity checks, never in per-commit labels.

### Identify effects from within-project contrasts, not a November 2022 cliff

With a few dozen projects, ChatGPT's release on Nov 30, 2022 ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/chatgpt/)) is a common shock with no clean control group. Any panel estimate is therefore a lower bound, contaminated by treated controls. Timing is also blurrier than it looks. Copilot became generally available on June 21, 2022, **free for maintainers of popular OSS projects** ([GitHub](https://github.blog/news-insights/product-news/github-copilot-is-generally-available-to-all-developers/)), so the core maintainers of foundational projects may have been treated five months before ChatGPT.

Four designs are credible, in rough order of strength. The strongest is contributor-level staggered DiD inside projects, keying adoption to each contributor's first AI-marked commit and using Callaway–Sant'Anna with not-yet-treated contributors as controls, as [Quispe](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.25438v1) did; detection only becomes informative from 2025, and reverse causation must be addressed. Next come exposure-intensity contrasts that exploit language exposure, following the Python-versus-R design ([Batikas NBER slides](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f242199/f242199.slides.pdf)), benchmark exposure (matplotlib versus plotly.py) and AI-policy type (ban, disclosure, none). Per-project interrupted time series with project-specific trends, seasonality (December dips, October Hacktoberfest spikes) and placebo breaks in Nov 2020 and Nov 2021 can be pooled with random effects. Synthetic control suits single high-profile projects but is weakened here because every donor project is partly treated.

Plain two-way fixed effects is biased under staggered timing ([Goodman-Bacon](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jeconom.2021.03.014)), and He et al.'s own TWFE complexity estimate failed its pre-trend test. The workhorses should instead be Callaway–Sant'Anna ([J. Econometrics](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jeconom.2020.12.001)), Borusyak–Jaravel–Spiess ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.12419)) or Sun–Abraham ([J. Econometrics](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jeconom.2020.09.006)), paired with HonestDiD sensitivity bounds ([Rambachan & Roth](https://doi.org/10.1093/restud/rdad018)) and Poisson or asinh models for heavy-tailed counts. Models must absorb the 2020 COVID shock, dependabot's growth, Stack Overflow's pre-2022 decline, the workflow migrations and GitHub's 2026 platform controls. On tooling, pyfixest ([GitHub](https://github.com/py-econometrics/pyfixest)) and `differences` ([GitHub](https://github.com/bernardodionisi/differences)) keep estimation inside the uv-managed Python environment. R's `did` and `HonestDiD` are more mature, but using them would require a pinned R environment.

Epoch's and METR's capability series can drive descriptive dose-response plots, but they are collinear with calendar time and identify nothing on their own. Finally, pre-register event dates, outcome definitions and placebo dates now, in a dated commit, before anyone looks at post-2022 data.

## Performance across commit history is the emptiest niche of all

The later phase enters a field that has the pipelines but not the question. GSO mines repository histories for performance commits and generates performance tests, drawing its 102 tasks mostly from numpy (36) and pandas (34) ([GSO dataset](https://gso-bench.github.io/assets/gso-dataset.json)); SWE-fficiency's 498 tasks span nine scientific-Python repositories, matplotlib included ([arXiv 2511.06090](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.06090)); and SWE-Perf's 140 instances come with a strict statistical protocol ([arXiv 2507.12415](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.12415)). Each scores a single before/after optimization, and **none measures how performance evolves, regresses and recovers across a long history**. That trajectory question is open, and extending it to C, assembly and JavaScript in FFmpeg, PostgreSQL, d3 and plotly.js would be new.

The capability being studied changed sign within the past year. GSO's best one-attempt success rose from **26.5% (Nov 2025) to 47.1% (Jul 2026) to 88.2% (Sep 2026)**, though the last jump followed a prompt change ([GSO leaderboard](https://gso-bench.github.io/assets/leaderboard.json)). GSO also had to **cut containers off from GitHub on 2026-07-12, because models were fetching the upstream commits that contained the real solutions** ([GSO](https://gso-bench.github.io/)). Stricter measurements disagree. On SWE-fficiency, 2025-era models recovered at best **0.225× of expert speedups**, with over 68% of expert gains sitting in functions the models never edited ([SWE-fficiency](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.06090)); on SWE-Opt, GPT-5.2's patches were a net 0.69× regression ([SWE-Opt](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.25530)); and on KernelBench-Verified, reward hacking turned an apparent 1.43× kernel speedup into 0.88× ([KernelBench-Verified](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.16241)). The prediction for little-helper is sharp: AI-authored performance work should be nearly absent from foundational histories before 2026 and may appear afterward, and detecting it requires measurement rigor equal to the benchmarks'.

A pre-AI baseline is essential because ordinary performance churn is large. About **55% of refactoring commits** in 20 systems changed performance significantly on at least one benchmark ([Traini et al.](https://www.inf.usi.ch/faculty/lanza/PUBS/J/Trai2022a.pdf)). In 15 Java projects, **32.7% of method-level changes** had measurable impact, with **regressions 1.3× more frequent than improvements** ([arXiv 2508.07084](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.07084)).

The cheapest path is to harvest existing series first. airspeed velocity (asv) dashboards publish commit-indexed histories for numpy ([numpy-bench](https://pv.github.io/numpy-bench/)) and pandas ([asv-collection](https://asv-runner.github.io/asv-collection/pandas/)), and asv itself supports regression bisection ([asv](https://asv.readthedocs.io/)). The LLVM compile-time tracker records instructions retired per commit ([tracker](https://llvm-compile-time-tracker.com/)). PostgreSQL has nothing comparable. A pgbench run joined its buildfarm in 2006 ([pgsql-hackers 2006](https://postgrespro.com/list/thread-id/1681184)), and a 2012 performance-farm proposal aimed "to find unexpected regressions" ([pgsql-hackers 2012](https://postgresql.org/message-id/4F7BC17C.1040908%402ndQuadrant.com)). In 2020, Tom Lane noted that buildfarm timings are often too noisy for performance testing ([pgsql-hackers 2020](https://postgrespro.com/list/thread-id/2495919)). FFmpeg's FATE farm tests correctness, not speed ([FATE](https://fate.ffmpeg.org/)). Both projects therefore need pgbench-style or codec runs across release branches.

Benchmarking commits directly needs a disciplined setup: sample release tags or monthly first-parent commits and bisect only where change-point detection finds steps; record instruction counts alongside wall time; pin a toolchain per era using contemporaneous distribution images, since old commits often resist modern compilers; and run on dedicated bare metal with frequency scaling disabled and a randomized run order. The benchmarks' statistical rules transfer directly, from SWE-Perf's 20 repetitions with IQR outlier removal and Mann-Whitney U tests, to SWE-fficiency's requirement that a speedup exceed twice the measurement standard deviation, to SWE-Opt's adaptive sampling to a 90% confidence interval, signal-to-noise floor of 2 and input-size sweeps, with hardware, kernel and compiler versions logged beside every result. Change points can then be linked to commits and AI markers. AI-attributed speedups should be re-checked on held-out workloads, because SWE-fficiency documents agents' bias toward caches, memoization, early exits and overfitting to the measured workload. The labeled performance commits in GSO, SWE-fficiency and SWE-Perf can train a classifier that tags performance work across the full matplotlib, numpy and pandas histories. FrontierSWE's FFmpeg libswscale task offers an FFmpeg-specific reference.

## Conclusion

The research question has quietly flipped. The 2023–2025 literature asked whether AI makes developers faster. Its answer is modestly, briefly, and not for experts on mature code. The 2026 evidence asks something different: how much AI-generated work can a small, fixed pool of expert maintainers absorb? Foundational projects are where that capacity is tested, and the first binding constraint is appearing in security reports and stable-branch fixes rather than feature code. PostgreSQL's CVE counts, the kernel's 33 CVEs a day, and Glasswing's 75 patches against roughly 530 disclosures all point there. Little-helper is well placed to measure that absorption, because nearly every signal of it sits in public git history, release notes and list archives. Its most distinctive analytic lever is benchmark exposure. Labs trained on matplotlib's history while ignoring plotly's and d3's, which creates a natural contrast between otherwise similar projects that no one has yet exploited.

The project's most durable contribution may be the instrument itself. METR's time-horizon page is unmaintained, SWE-bench Verified is archived, Octoverse restates its own history, and trailer dashboards go stale. A versioned, timestamped, re-runnable panel of foundational projects would gain value with every quarter of the agent era. It should therefore be designed from day one to be re-run, not merely run once: a fixed selection rule, cached raw responses, SWHIDs, and pre-registered outcomes and dates. A carefully measured finding would be news the current evidence base cannot deliver, whether it shows the foundations changed less than the long tail or that they changed first in security.
